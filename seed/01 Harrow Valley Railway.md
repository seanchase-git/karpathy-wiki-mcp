# Harrow Valley Railway

Source: Harrow Valley Historical Society pamphlet (1998)

## Overview

The Harrow Valley Railway was a 2 ft 6 in narrow-gauge line running 19 miles from the market town of Tallow Bridge up the valley to the Dunmoor Mine, high on the Mirren Fells. It was built to carry copper and tin ore down to the smelter at Tallow Bridge, and to bring coal, timber and machinery back up to the workings. Passengers were an afterthought at first, but by the 1890s the Saturday market train was a fixture of valley life.

## Construction and opening

The line was financed by the Calder-Voss Mining Company, which had struggled for years with pack ponies and wagons on the old Dunmoor road. Surveying began in 1871 under the company's newly hired engineer, Ingrid Halvorsen Pell, and construction started the following spring. The railway opened on 14 March 1874, when the first ore train left Dunmoor behind a locomotive named "Mirren". Mrs Pell chose the narrow gauge deliberately: it allowed tighter curves and cheaper earthworks in a valley where the river and the road left very little room.

## Engineering

The line's defining feature was the Sable Gap switchback. Between the village of Sable Gap and the Mirren Fells the valley wall rises 340 feet in under a mile, too steep for a conventional continuous climb. Pell solved this with a switchback grade: the track zig-zags up the slope in three legs, with trains reversing at two short dead-end spurs. The arrangement was slow but reliable, and it needed no special locomotives.

By the late 1880s ore traffic had outgrown the switchback. In 1891 Pell designed a replacement for the steepest stretch at Gannet Bank, a 1-in-6 incline worked by a rack railway system. A toothed rail laid between the running rails engaged a cog wheel on the locomotive, allowing heavy trains to climb without reversing. The Gannet Bank rack section, known locally as the "Pell rack", cut the journey time from Dunmoor to Tallow Bridge by almost forty minutes.

## Operations

At its peak in the 1900s the railway operated eleven locomotives and about 400 wagons. Ore trains ran daily, with a mixed passenger service three days a week. The main station at Tallow Bridge sat beside the river, next to the smelter yard and the engineering shops of Pell & Daughters.

## Decline and preservation

When the Dunmoor Mine closed in 1934, the railway lost its main traffic. It limped on with timber and a reduced passenger service until final closure in 1951, and the track was lifted above Sable Gap soon afterwards.

In 1978 a group of enthusiasts founded the Harrow Valley Railway Preservation Society. Volunteers relaid the lower six miles from Tallow Bridge, and the heritage line reopened to the public in 1986. The Sable Gap switchback has not been restored, but its earthworks remain visible and are a stop on the Tallow Bridge heritage trail. The society's collection includes "Mirren", which was recovered from a quarry siding in 1980.
